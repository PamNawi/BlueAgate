Presented here are some hopefully helpful civic numbers, sizes and metrics. Keep in mind that all metrics are subject to different engineering and estimation techniques per country, differing historical needs, and that sometimes the categories presented here are very porous.

Remember that the population size differences between a village, a town, a city, and a metropolis are not the sole differentiating factor between urban formations. The variety of the included or supported functions, the urban structures, and the civic densities play a crucial role too. A few houses close to each other, for example, do not constitute a settlement. The same few houses organized around a church and a tavern (usually) do.

# POPULATIONS
- Village population: up to 5,000
- Small town: 5,000 to 10,000
- Town: 10,000 to 100,000
- Small city: 50,000 to 200,000
- Medium sized cities: 200,000 to 500,000
- Large cities: 400,000 to 1,500,000
- Metropolis: above 1,000,000. Usually above 2,000,000.

The megalopolis is a more or less theoretical concept of cities so thoroughly connected they can be approached and planned as a single unit.

# ARCHITECTURAL METRICS
- Average floor height of a contemporary building would be 4 meters (13 feet). 3,5 meters isn't an uncommon height.
- Apartment buildings can be as small as 10x10 m. They can be high-rise, mid-rise (6 to 14 floors in many countries), or low-rise. Residential towers in NYC often exceed 60 floors and have actually reached 113 floors.
- A Gothic cathedral's towers can rise up to 140m. Floor to ceiling heights can reach 50m.
- A football stadium (of around 15,000 spectators) would roughly have these dimensions: 180x160 m.

# STREETS AND PAVEMENTS
- Average contemporary street lanes range between 3 to 4.5 meters (9.8 to 14.8 feet).
- City street widths often depend on local transportation tech -- a loaded donkey or camel, a tram, a car, a pedestrian etc should be able to traverse them.
- Contemporary local roads can be as narrow as 1 to 2 lanes, while highways can extend to up to 32 lanes (or even more in extreme/silly cases).
- Bicycle lanes have a minimum width of 1.5m. 2m widths are considered safer though.
- Sidewalk heights should not exceed 20cm, and 12-15cm heights are generally comfortable. Sidewalks widths are often proportional to the road they accompany. Still, a comfortable sidewalk should allow for two citizens to walk abreast.
- Roundabouts range from 15m diameter (mini) to 150m (multi-level)
- Traffic signals are 4.5 to 6m high (mounted), or 2.5 to 4m (standing)
- The ideal enclosure sense for a pedestrian is achieved with a building height to road width ratio of 1:1. 4:1 is considered claustrophobic whereas 1:4 would be too formless to feel meaningfully urban.
- Manhattan's average road widths: avenues (30.5 m), standard street (18.3 m); slightly narrower in lower Manhattan.
- Highway interchanges can cover up to 3 square kilometers.
- Railroad tracks are usually 1.5 m wide.
- Tram lanes (single) are on average 3.3 to 3.4 meters in width.

# TELECOMUNICATIONS, POWER, STREET FURNITURE

- Telephone poles standard height in the US is 10 m. In extreme cases poles can reach heights of up to 40m.
- Mobile phone towers: macro cell tower ~ 120m, macro tower on building ~ 30m, small cell antenna ~ 10m.
- Bell tower: the highest is 113.3 meters (Mortegliano Bell Tower, Italy)
- Telephone booths 1x1x2.5m (standard UK ones)
- Benches: 1.5 m to 1.7 m length, 0.70-0.85 m maximum height, 35 to 45 cm sitting space width. Obviously countless variations can be encountered.
- Street lamps range from 6 (local roads) to 12 meters (boulevards)
- Street signs height at around 2.1 m
