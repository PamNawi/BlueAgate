# Urban Functions & Land Use
Urban functions are the why a city exist and what they do to serve that purpose. A city will evolve to facilitate and support those functions, and will also change as those functions also evolve. Its common for games to ignore the urban functions on games, making they feel less believable, aesthetically disappointing, paper-thin, or incomplete settlements . Yet when its considered the urban functions a city will appear more interesting and even more fertile.

**List of Contemporary Urban Functions:** residence, commerce, production, consumption, human, reproduction, security, communication, education, transportation, culture, ideology, the circulation of capital, administration, socialization and access to drinking water and energy

**Medieval settlement:** defense and religion. The need for water would be satisfied through wells and nearby lakes, and not through a network of running water pipes or bottled water.

**Deep space settlement:** shielding from radiation and artificial gravity could also be functions. Would still have any religious function inside that settlement?

You also should be considering how the communication and transport will be done since they determine the physical limits of urbanism. Depending of the functions, its possible to use those to differentiate between settlements inside the same world. By varying the functions an open world map can have a feel of not being populated with similar-but-reskinned towns and cities. Example:

- Examples of functions that would imply a complex geographical location: An exploration outpost, a heavily industrialized city, a fortified outpost, a capital focused on administration, a mining village and a specialized transportation hub.

Notice that smaller cities normally have a single function (defined by economy normally), while metropolis will aggregate over the time a multitude of functions.

As always forms follows function, so  urban and architectural spaces of games do not serve the functions and needs of actual humans or societies; they serve the demands of a game's fiction and gameplay.

To balance fiction and mechanics, and to support the suspension of disbelief, it helps if the game functions of spaces are reminiscent of functions of represented spaces. Changes and variations are obviously allowed.

Aligning game & real-life functions makes game cities more convincing, more legible, and thus easier to actually use. Like on World of Warcraft this Horde settlement, with the architectural elements sell to the player that area is a commercial zone where the player is invited explore and talk with the NPCs. On Frostpunk, one of the main mechanics is that there's a permanent winter, so the city need to have those big pyres to heat around the buildings.

# Functions and Urban Land Uses
All the urban functions a city has take space and use the land. This mean that we can organize and plan how the space will used. The space that cities reserve for specific activities and functions is usually described as 'land use'. Land use planning or zoning is the spatial organization of said uses. A typical land use plan looks like this:

![[Pasted image 20260318135240.png]]

We need to know where our virtual people live, and where they work. Where they shop, where their parks their cars and places of assembly are they entertain themselves, where they bury their dead, where they travel to and from... we can place all into a single map showing where all the urban functions take.

How we plan the usage of the land also change depending of when the city is placed. Districts, a core element of all urban space, and the expression of any city's internal differentiation, are to a great part determined from their dominant land uses (as well as factors such as class, age, and location). Typical districts of the modern city include suburbia, the Central Business District, Exurbia, the historical centre, several types of residential districts, industrial districts, and more.


The land use categories we tend to find in a modern city are varied, and usually differ between planning / zoning system. We generally reserve space for several types of residential purposes, industry, offices, administration, parks, open spaces, transportation, facilities, security services such as police and fire departments, hospital and social welfare.

- We also reserve space for religion, warehouses, commerce (retailing and wholesale), light industry, education, farming, logistics, public spaces and even unused land.
- More specific land uses such as slaughterhouses, greenbelts, monuments, archeological sites, private gardens, agricultural land or construction sites can also be added in a land use plan.

Districts can be homogenous, depending on the urban functions they will have, but don't have to be reserved for a single land use exclusively. Mixed use is common both historically and in contemporary cities. Restaurants and shops, for example, are routinely found in residential areas.

Homogenous areas also exist. Typical suburbia is almost exclusively residential, and most industrial zones are predominantly productive.

Land uses can also be described by their radius, which can range from local to neighborhood, to district, to city wide and even to national or global. Land uses with a greater radius have to be accessible by more people, and thus tend to be both more centrally located, and/or in areas served by good transportation networks.

Its is common to ssign land use per block, and district. It is also very common to assign land use by street. We can have a commercial street in an otherwise residential area, and describe a street as commercial, ceremonial or neighborhood-level.
