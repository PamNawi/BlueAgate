# Urban Functions & Land Use
Urban functions are the why a city exist and what they do to serve that purpose. A city will evolve to facilitate and support those functions, and will also change as those functions also evolve through time. Its common for games to ignore the urban functions, making they feel less believable, as aesthetically disappointing, creating the sensation of incomplete settlements . Yet when its considered the urban functions a city will appear more interesting and even more fertile. Its important too that we can also use those to functions to define the limits of districts inside each city.

**List of Contemporary Urban Functions:** residence, commerce, production, consumption, human, reproduction, security, communication, education, transportation, culture, ideology, the circulation of capital, administration, socialization and access to drinking water and energy

**List of Medieval Settlement Functions:** defense and religion. The need for water would be satisfied through wells and nearby lakes, and not through a network of running water pipes or bottled water.

**List of Deep Space Settlement Functions:** shielding from radiation and artificial gravity could also be functions. 
- Would still have any religious function inside that settlement? It will depends on the culture of the people on that settlement
- Consider alien species here

The way that people will communicate and transport inside the limits of the city also determine the physical limits of urbanism. Remember of the ["Porcelain Hypothesis"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L0qh-sxXe1c) in both cases.
- How people are traversing their city will define how far they can go inside the city.
- How people are communicating also implies the technology of the era. They are using handwritten letters? E-mails? SMS? Telephone Cabins? Mental link connections...? 
- Depending of the specific function of the city, on why she exists, is possible to differentiate between settlements inside the same world.  Example of functions that would imply a complex geographical location: An exploration outpost, a heavily industrialized city, a fortified outpost, a capital focused on administration, a mining village and a specialized transportation hub.
- Smaller cities normally have a single function (defined by economy normally), while metropolis will aggregate over the time a multitude of functions. 

**"As always forms follows function, so  urban and architectural spaces of games do not serve the functions and needs of actual humans or societies; they serve the demands of a game's fiction and gameplay."**

If the game functions are reminiscent of functions of the space that is represented is easier to help the illusion of city. By aligning in game functions and & real-life functions is easier to make the city more convincing, legible and of course easier to use.

- On World of Warcraft this Horde settlement, has architectural elements that sell to the players that this area is a commercial zone. The players naturally will explore and talk with the NPCs.
-  On Frostpunk, one of the main mechanics is that there's a permanent winter, so the city need to have those big pyres to heat around the buildings.

**All the urban functions of a city will take space and use the land.** 
This mean that we can organize and plan how the space will used. How the city will reserve their space for specific activities and functions is usually described as 'land use'. A typical land use plan looks like this:

This planning will answer some questions?
- Where the virtual people live?
- Where they work? They work on what?
- Where they can shop?
- Where are they can park their cars?
- Where they can entertain themselves?
- Where they assembly?

- How the technology is affecting the people on this city (shaping the life of the people)? Again the ["Porcelain Hypothesis"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L0qh-sxXe1c) came to my mind.

We can combine all of this information in a single map showing all the urban functions present on it. The creation of districts can help on the differentiation of the functions present on a area by showing their dominant land use (consider also other factions as class, age, location).
- Typical districts of the modern city include: Suburbia, the Central Business District, [Exurbia](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ex%C3%BArbio), the historical center, several types of residential districts, industrial districts, and more.
- The land use categories we tend to find in a modern city are varied, and usually differ between planning / zoning system. We generally reserve space for several types of residential purposes, industry, offices, administration, parks, open spaces, transportation, facilities, security services such as police and fire departments, hospital and social welfare.
- We also reserve space for religion, warehouses, commerce (retailing and wholesale), light industry, education, farming, logistics, public spaces and even unused land.
- More specific land uses such as slaughterhouses, greenbelts, monuments, archeological sites, private gardens, agricultural land or construction sites can also be added in a land use plan.

The creation of a district can be following the path of creating something homogenous, or mixed. How homogenous or mixed the district will be is determined by the functions that it provide and how people will traverse it. You can have a residential area with many restaurants and shops, its historically accurate for the contemporary cities. Yet you can have a typical suburbia with exclusively residential zones or industrial zones who are predominantly productive depending on how people will access those regions.

You should also consider how far that function will affect/act. Consider the range (radius) of that land use: local neighborhood, district, city wide, national or global. Examples:
- A cafeteria will serve a local neighborhood
- An airport can have a global relevance
- A market can serve a district
- A specific cultural district might serve a whole megalopolis ([Liberdade on São Paulo](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberdade_(bairro_de_S%C3%A3o_Paulo)) which is a Japanese district very distinct of the rest of the city)

Land uses with a greater radius have to be accessible by more people, and thus tend to be both more centrally located, and/or in areas served by good transportation networks. Its is common to assign land use per block, and district. It is also very common to assign land use by street. We can have a commercial street in an otherwise residential area, and describe a street as commercial, ceremonial or neighborhood-level.

# District Types
There's some typical districts that can be seem in many cities:
- Central Business District (CBD): Usually part of the modern downtown, tends to be characterized by high building density and concentrate commercial, business and leisure activities.
- Suburbia: Low-density areas, commonly on the outskirts of a city, where the single-family home is dominant land use. A few schools, and supporting shopping areas are often planned for it.
- Exurbia: The area beyond the suburbs that is still functionally connected to the city. Typically includes farmland, edge cities, industrial and vacationing areas.

# City Structures
The urban structure can be described as the spatial distribution and organization of the land in the space, its shapes the urban form, and often is shaped by the geography. It doesn't include details like the shape of a road or architectural styles of each area. There's many models that where proposed a long the history:

**Concentric Zone Model:** Its also know as as the burgess model and was created by Ernest Burgess in 1925. It was created based on human ecology theory and was applied on Chicago. The idea is that the CDB was the center of the model and then concentric rings are expanded around it. 

![[Pasted image 20260318142313.png]]

This model don't work much outside the United States and even inside the States since the cities transformed with the advancement of transportation and the information technology, making the cities not so easy to be organized with clear "zones".

**Sector Model:** On 1939 Homer Hoyt proposed a modification on the Burgess model, creating the sector model. The advantage of this model compared with the concentric one is that allow for an outward progression and growth. This model applies to numerous British cities and if its turned 90 degrees counter-clockwise it will fits the city of [Mönchengladbach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M%C3%B6nchengladbach "Mönchengladbach").

![[Pasted image 20260318142343.png]]

The theory is based on early twentieth century rail transport and does not make allowances for private cars that enable commuting from cheaper land outside city boundaries. This theory also does not take into account the new concepts of edge cities and boomburbs, which began to emerge on 1980s

**Multiple Nuclei Model:** Proposed by Chauncy Harris and Edward Ullman in 1945 on an article called "The Nature of Cities". This model is based on Chicago arguing that even the city begun with a defined a single and central CBD, as the time passed, other smallers CBDs where develop on the outskirts near the more valuable housing areas allowing shorter commutes from the outskirts.

![[Pasted image 20260318142409.png]]

The main goals on this model were:
- Move from the concentric zone model
- Better reflect  the complex nature of urban areas, especially those of larger size

### Other structural models:
**Garden City:** The garden city movement was a 20th century movement that promoted satellite communities surrounding the central city, separated by green belts. These garden cities would contain proportionate areas of residences, industry and agriculture.

**Linear Cities:** A linear settlement or group of buildings that is formed is a long line. Many of these settlements are formed along a transport route, such as a road, river or canal. Others form due to physical restrictions, such coastlines, mountains, hills or valleys. Its important to notice that those settlements don't form a obvious centre.

# The City Centre
The dominant urban functions and ideology, and their assorted land uses should dominate the city center of any memorable urban agglomeration.

- City centers often include the oldest / initial sections of a city, and are thus places where civic history can be showcased
- Centers tend to be the points of maximum density and maximum accessibility, and also highly interconnected with the rest of the city.
- A function / land use that appeals to all the city tends to be placed towards the center to ensure maximum approachability, and benefit from the central richness in infrastructure and connections.
- The modern city center is more complex and varied than the rest of the urban tissue on average. Its houses the functions the current socioeconomic system deems as most valuable: finance, corporate HQs and administration.
- City centers often feature impressive architecture public spaces & buildings ideologically significant edifices, quality plans and designs, as well some of the residences of the elite. They characterize the city.
- Every city has to end at some point; it needs boundaries. City limits are not always as rigids as one would imagine, and always pose questions for open world games.
	- Walls, fortifications, defense lines, minefields
	- Roadblocks, collapsed tunnels/bridges, impassable traffic
	- Bodies of water, mountain ranges, chasms, deadly environments
	- Enchanted forests, impossible labyrinths, Ravenloft's fog
	- Train-lines, highways
	- Continuous buildings / Factories /Fences
	- Unending procedural ruins, deserts, etcs
	- The (popular) island and mechanical limits (starvation, enemies, fuel)
	- Extreme danger (like snipers shooting from a tower)

# Qualities of the Virtual Public Space
Creating virtual public spaces is very costly, since they normally need to be supported by NPCs, they will probably need some special models and texture for the area, possible many light sources and so so. This means that we should take special care to those spaces, making them memorable, with many ways of being explored and worth of the being revisited. Always consider the following when building a public space in a game:
- Legibility
- Ease of navigation
- Vibrancy - the sense of a crowd
- Interesting traversal
- Clarity of function
- Uniqueness, intrigue and memorability

# Level Design and City Form
As always form should always follow function, this means that the shape of the city and the structure of it should be both informing and supporting gameplay. There will be a constant dialogue between the geometry of the setting and level design requirements, one influence the other.

Consider arenas, hiding places, views, snipping points, social interactions, racing lanes, access to resources or shops, barricades, lighting, parkour, lines of sight, and accordingly decide to curve roads, play with the quality of the lighting, block off streets or tamper with building heights and park widths.

# Infrastructure
  Infrastructure is the skeleton of the city, which allows it to exist, survive, and carry out its day-to-day functions. Infrastructure is closely tied with the overall urban functions, as well as the inflexible needs of cities. The urban assets comprising urban infrastructure are the nerve centers and neuralgic points of any city.

- Infrastructure evolves through history, and as both human needs and technologies evolve.
- Infrastructure covers the crucial and very broad sectors of: transportation, communication, waste management, sanitation, water management, power, defense, flood and fire management, public health, and more.
- Infrastructure ensures the access to (and circulation of) drinkable water via assets such as: aqueducts, cisterns, wells, water towers, dams, reservoirs, water treatment facilities and pipe networks

# Creating wider Geographies & Hinterlands
Natural, human, economic, and (geo)political geographies determine many aspects of any given city, as well as it surroundings. The geography of settlements of any given country, nation or even empire is determined by its natural geography, and regional planning systems. Walter Christaller's Central Place Theory can provide imaginary geographers with a good starting point:

![[Pasted image 20260409200205.png]]

Urban hinterlands describe the geography of the areas immediately adjacent to a city; the surrounding areas that are closely and functionally linked to the city itself. Hinterlands are the backyard lands of a city, and can include smaller settlements, rural areas, mines, farmland, etc.

This can be seen on the density map of Europe, while o France and England we have both countries concentrating many people on their capitals, you can see how Germany spread their population.

![[Pasted image 20260409200816.png]]

Try to avoid geographical determinism; if nothing else it often leads to dull predictable places.

There's a direct correlation between settlement function and settlement size. A tiny village, for example, would not be able to support a university or even feature a cinema.

The uniqueness of each settlement, even among very similar ones, has to be maintained when designing larger worlds; uniqueness can often even be emphasized to compensate for the attention spans of all types of players. Note that larger settlements have a distinctive physiognomies.

When referring to the hinterland of a city, we are referring to the surrounding areas that are closely linked to the city itself. Hinterlands can include settlements, towns, rural areas, mines, farmlands, etc.

Hinterlands are effectively the backyard lands of a city; places inter-dependent or directly controlled by the city.
